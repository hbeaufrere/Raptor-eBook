# Golden eagle

_Aquila chrysaetos_

## Description

### General Appearance

The Golden Eagle is a very large, heavy-bodied, dark brown raptor with long, broad wings (length 70 to 99 cm; wingspan 185 to 222 cm). It soars with wings in a slight dihedral and does not rock like a Turkey Vulture. The **golden-brown lanceolate feathers of the rear crown, nape, and sides of the neck** give the species its name and are present at all ages. The main confusion species is the immature Bald Eagle: the Golden Eagle has a smaller head and bill (the head projects less than half the tail length in flight), **fully feathered tarsi** (the Bald Eagle's are bare), a yellow cere at all ages, and white in the plumage that is cleanly placed (base of flight feathers and tail) rather than patchy on the body and underwings.

### Adult Plumage

Adults (older than about 4 to 5 years) are predominantly dark brown with faint gray marbling and bars on the flight feathers and tail, golden hackles on the nape, and paler, tawny upperwing coverts forming a diagonal bar visible on flying and perched birds. Fresh feathers are dark and glossy; older feathers fade to brown or yellowish-white, so adults typically show a mosaic of feather generations. Some individuals carry small white scapular "epaulets" (the Barthelemyi variant).

### Juvenile and Subadult Plumages

Juveniles are uniformly dark, with all feathers the same generation, and usually show **white at the base of the inner primaries and secondaries (forming a white wing "window") and a white-based tail with a sharply defined wide dark terminal band**. The amount of white varies and a few juveniles lack it. White diminishes with each successive molt through the second and third basic plumages, which are recognizable by mixed generations of remiges (typically 3 to 7 retained juvenile outer primaries in the second basic plumage, and 1 to 3, usually among p8 to p10, in the third). Definitive adult plumage is usually attained at the fourth or fifth basic plumage, at 4 to 5 years of age. Aging by plumage and molt is far more reliable than by the amount of white alone.

### Differences Between Sexes

Sexes are similar in plumage, but **females are substantially larger and heavier than males** (male mass roughly 2.4 to 4.5 kg; female 3.0 to 6.4 kg). Wing chord and mass alone are unreliable for sexing; **foot pad length is highly accurate (males under 138.5 mm, females over 138.5 mm in one Idaho study)**, and combinations of culmen and hallux claw length sex 97 to 100 percent of birds correctly. Adult females may show fewer, wider tail bands than the multiple narrow bands of males, though this correctly assigns sex only about 70 percent of the time.

### Bare Parts

The bill is tricolored: black at the tip, fading to gray at the base, with a yellow cere. The iris is dark brown in juveniles and varies in adults from dark brown or hazel to flecked gold; eye color is not a reliable age indicator. Legs are feathered to the toes; the feet are pale to bright yellow with black talons.

### Subspecies

Six subspecies are recognized worldwide across the Holarctic range; all North American birds belong to **A. c. canadensis**.

## Distribution and Migration

### Overall Range

The Golden Eagle has a Holarctic distribution spanning North America, Eurasia, and parts of North Africa. In North America it breeds across Alaska and Canada and through the western half of the continent (west of the 100th meridian) south to central Mexico. Northern populations are migratory; southern breeders, including California birds, are essentially **resident, holding territories year-round**, though residents range more widely in the nonbreeding season and some southwestern subadults make northward summer movements of hundreds of kilometers. The United States population is estimated at about 40,000 individuals, and recent analyses suggest western populations are stable to slightly declining, held below carrying capacity by human-caused mortality.

### California

Golden Eagles nest through much of California's foothill and mountain terrain, avoiding the immediate coast, the flat floor of the Central Valley, and the southeastern deserts during nesting. Grassland and oak savanna foothills support some of the **highest nesting densities recorded anywhere (about 18.5 km2 per pair in central California's Diablo Range)**. California history illustrates the pressures on the species: nesting pairs in San Diego County fell from an estimated 85 pairs in 1900 to about 40 occupied territories by 1999, with further losses since, driven by residential development. Wind energy is a major regional mortality source, most notoriously at the Altamont Pass Wind Resource Area east of San Francisco Bay. Drought in the Diablo Range does not reduce territory occupancy but strongly reduces the probability of successful nesting. Knemidocoptic mange caused by a newly described mite has injured and killed eagles in California. Clinically important admissions include **lead poisoning (from scavenging shot carcasses), rodenticide exposure, electrocution, vehicle and turbine collisions, and trichomonosis in birds feeding on pigeons**. The species is protected federally under the Bald and Golden Eagle Protection Act and is a California Fully Protected species, so all handling, rehabilitation, and placement decisions carry additional regulatory requirements.

## Diet and Biology

### Diet

During the nesting season in western North America the diet is dominated by **mammals (84 percent of prey items), mainly leporids (jackrabbits and cottontails) and sciurids (ground squirrels, marmots, prairie dogs)**, with birds (15 percent, chiefly gallinaceous birds and waterfowl) secondary. In Mediterranean California, California ground squirrels are a primary prey. Preferred prey mass is roughly 0.5 to 4 kg. The species **scavenges extensively on carrion, especially in winter**, which is the main route of lead and rodenticide exposure. Predation on livestock is rare (livestock remains, mostly scavenged, made up only 1.4 percent of over 7,000 prey items in western studies). Diet shifts with prey availability: after wildfires destroyed jackrabbit habitat in Idaho, waterfowl rose from under 2 percent to 27 percent of the diet.

### Foraging Behavior

Golden Eagles hunt from soaring flight, from low contouring flight that uses terrain to surprise prey, and from perches (perch hunting predominates where perches are abundant). Seven stereotyped attack techniques are described, from long low-angle glides and vertical stoops to the "walk and grab" used on ground squirrel colonies and the sustained-grip attack used on ungulates. Mated pairs sometimes hunt in tandem, one bird diverting prey while the other strikes, though solo hunts are actually more successful (29 percent versus about 5 percent in Idaho; overall capture success about 20 percent). Pairs deliver roughly 0.9 to 1.5 kg of prey biomass per day to a nest, and an estimated 24 to 33 kg of food is needed to raise one nestling to fledging. Free-flying birds consume about 6 percent of body mass per day; nestlings 12 to 15 percent.

### Reproduction

The Golden Eagle is monogamous, long-lived, and slowly reproducing, with pair bonds and territories maintained across years. In California, courtship occurs from December to January and nest building begins in fall; the whole cycle from early territory defense to independence of young can span nearly 12 months in resident populations. Nests are massive stick structures on cliffs, in large trees, or occasionally on the ground or structures; territories typically contain several alternative nests. Clutch size is typically 2 (range 1 to 3, rarely 4; 76 percent of 332 western clutches held 2 eggs), laid at 3 to 5 day intervals. **Incubation, mostly by the female, averages 42 days** (range 41 to 45). Renesting after failure is rare. Hatching is asynchronous, and broods of one fledged young are the norm (about 63 percent of broods); siblicide and starvation of the junior chick occur when food is short. Hatchlings weigh about 110 g; female nestlings reach about 3.8 kg and males 3.2 kg. Young begin self-feeding at 34 to 37 days, make their first flight at roughly 9 to 11 weeks, and in California and other resident populations may remain in the natal territory until fall or even the following spring. Long-term productivity is at most about 1 fledgling per territorial pair per year, and most birds first breed at 4 to 5 years or later, key numbers when considering the population impact of each adult mortality.

## Molt

The Golden Eagle exhibits a **Modified Basic Strategy** with incomplete prebasic molts in most individuals, a limited preformative molt in some, and no prealternate molt. There are 10 primaries, 14 to 15 secondaries plus 3 tertials, and 12 rectrices.

The prejuvenile molt in the nest is the **only complete molt this species ever undergoes**; juvenile feathering is essentially complete at about 60 days, with full feather growth by 80 to 105 days. Thereafter, each prebasic molt (roughly March or April through October to December) is incomplete, and **2 to 3 years are needed to replace a full feather set**, so adults always carry 2 to 4 generations of flight feathers replaced in stepwise (Staffelmauser) waves. Primaries are replaced from p1 distally, secondaries from s1 and s5 and from the tertials, and rectrices most often in the sequence r1, r6, r3, r4, r2, r5 with much variation. Second and third prebasic molts leave predictable sets of retained juvenile feathers (see Plumages above) that allow precise aging in the hand through about 4 to 5 years of age. Unlike many raptors, breeding Golden Eagles do not clearly suspend molt during brood rearing, and winter molt of a few feathers is not uncommon. Because full replacement takes years, any feather damage (from trauma, bandaging, or cage wear) in a rehabilitation setting can persist across multiple seasons.
