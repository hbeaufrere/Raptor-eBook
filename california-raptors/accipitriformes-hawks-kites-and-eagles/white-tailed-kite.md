# White-tailed kite

_Elanus leucurus_

## Description

### General Appearance

The White-tailed Kite is a medium-small, strikingly pale raptor, 32 to 38 cm in total length, with **long, somewhat pointed, relatively narrow wings and a long white tail** that extends well beyond the wingtips when perched. The overall impression is of a gull-like, luminous white bird with a gray back. Conspicuous in good light are the **large black scapular patches and marginal coverts on an otherwise gray back, and a black underwing covert patch near the bend of the wing (carpal area)**. In flight it is most easily identified by its hunting style: persistent hovering 5 to 25 m above open grassland, a behavior no other California raptor of this size and color shares. The adult male Northern Harrier is the main confusion species but shows black primaries below, courses low over the ground, and rarely hovers at kite heights.

### Adult Plumage

Adults are **pure white below and on the head, with a neutral gray back and upperwings from crown to upper tail coverts, black lesser and median wing coverts (the "black shoulder"), and a small black spot in front of the red eye**. The central rectrices are pale whitish-gray and the remainder of the tail is white. Sexes are similar, though females average slightly darker backed than males.

### Juvenile Plumage

Juveniles show **buffy to tawny streaking on the breast and head**, often forming a band across the upper breast, and a browner back with white-tipped (scalloped) feathers. The tail is pale gray with a narrow (about 5 mm) darker gray subterminal band. All juvenile feathering is acquired by about 40 days. In the first fall, a gradual body molt (into what is essentially adult-like plumage) begins on the head and mantle while the juvenile remiges and rectrices are retained; by December, first-year birds are difficult to distinguish from adults in the field.

### Differences Between Sexes

Unlike most raptors, **the sexes are similar in size**, and plumage differences are subtle (females slightly darker dorsally). This limits morphometric sexing in the clinic; DNA sexing is the reliable option when sex matters for management.

### Bare Parts

The bill is black at all ages with a red gape. The legs and feet are yellow at all ages and relatively short and small, reflecting the small-mammal diet. **The iris is brown in juveniles and ruby red in adults, with the color change occurring at about 6 months of age**, a useful rapid aging criterion on intake.

### Subspecies

Two subspecies are recognized: _E. l. majusculus_ in North and Central America (the larger form, and the one occurring in California) and the nominate _E. l. leucurus_ in South America. The species was long lumped with the Old World Black-shouldered Kite (_Elanus caeruleus_) before being re-split in the 1990s based on size, proportions, and the black underwing carpal patch.

## Distribution and Migration

### Overall Range

The White-tailed Kite occurs in open lowland habitats from the western and southern United States through Mexico and Central America, and across much of South America to central Chile and Argentina. The present distribution is the largest in the species' known history. In North America, **the breeding stronghold is California**; smaller populations occur in western Oregon, southwestern Washington, southern Texas, and southern Florida. The species is essentially **resident, but nomadic rather than truly migratory**: populations track cyclic vole abundance, with dispersal and local range shifts mostly between January and April, and dramatic irruptive range expansions documented over the past decades. Outside the breeding season, kites commonly gather at communal night roosts that may lie tens of kilometers from their daytime ranges.

### California

The species is **common in the Central Valley and along the entire length of the California coast**, extending up to the western Sierra Nevada foothills, with recent breeding even in more arid southern counties. Its California history is dramatic: by the 1930s, after habitat loss, shooting, and egg collecting, extinction in the state was widely predicted. The population then increased spectacularly from the 1940s to the early 1980s. More recent trends are again concerning, with Breeding Bird Survey data suggesting declines in many regions, steepest in southern California grasslands, linked to urban conversion of grasslands and agricultural land, clean farming practices that remove the residual grassy cover voles need, competition for nest sites, and drought. **The White-tailed Kite is a Fully Protected species under the California Fish and Game Code**, a designation that prohibits take and adds a regulatory layer relevant to rehabilitation and education placements.

## Diet and Biology

### Diet

The White-tailed Kite is a **small-mammal specialist to a degree unusual among raptors: in all 12 published diet studies, small mammals comprised more than 95 percent of prey items** (more than 12,500 prey identified from pellets). In California the California vole (Microtus californicus) is the staple; prey generally fall in the 20 to 70 g range. Birds, lizards, and insects are taken only incidentally. This near-total dependence on cyclic vole populations drives most of the species' biology, from territory size and breeding density to nomadism, and makes a whole-prey rodent diet the obvious choice in captivity.

### Foraging Behavior

The kite **hunts almost exclusively by hovering**, typically 5 to 25 m above the ground, facing into the wind and scanning below; hovers last from a second to over a minute and end with a feet-first, wings-up vertical drop onto prey, a move to a new hover point, or a return to a perch. Perch hunting is rare. Hunting bouts average 5 to 6 minutes, and roughly 40 to 50 percent of strikes yield prey. Hunting concentrates in the first and last hours of the day. After a capture, the bird usually makes a low direct flight to a favored perch, where it eviscerates the prey, eats the head, then consumes the body from the rear forward, scanning between bites. Kites are frequent victims of kleptoparasitism by harriers, Red-tailed Hawks, and ravens. Daily intake during the nonbreeding season has been estimated at about 3 prey per day, roughly 75 g of food.

### Reproduction

Pairs may remain together and defend territories year-round. Courtship occurs from December through September, and nest building (7 to 28 days, by both sexes, with dummy nests common) runs from January to August. The nest is a well-made structure of small twigs lined with grass or leaves, placed in the upper third of a tree; more than 20 nest tree species have been recorded, with tree structure mattering more than species. First eggs appear as early as the first week of February in southern California and mid to late March in northern California. The typical clutch is 4 eggs (range 3 to 6), white to creamy white and heavily blotched with rich browns, laid every other day. **Only the female incubates**, for 30 to 32 days, receiving prey from the male in perched or aerial exchanges in which she flies up and grasps the prey from his feet from behind. Hatching is asynchronous. Nestlings gain about 12 g per day, reach adult mass by the fourth week, and fledge at about 4 to 5 weeks; fledglings depend on the parents for another 1 to 2 months. Uniquely among California raptors, **pairs commonly attempt a second brood in years of high vole abundance**, sometimes starting a new nest while fledglings from the first are still dependent. Productivity averages roughly 1.6 young fledged per active nest and about 3 per successful nest.

## Molt

Molt in this species is comparatively simple and poorly studied. The juvenile plumage is complete by about 40 days. A first (preformative/basic I equivalent) body molt begins at roughly 3.5 to 6 months, proceeding from the head and mantle and producing adult-like body plumage while juvenile flight feathers are retained; this is why streaky-breasted young birds become hard to separate from adults by early winter. The definitive prebasic molt is **complete and prolonged over summer and fall**, with primaries renewed from innermost to outermost (p1 to p10). There is no prealternate molt. In the clinic, a bird with uniformly adult body plumage but retained, worn juvenile remiges and a trace of the narrow gray subterminal tail band can be aged as a first-year bird.
