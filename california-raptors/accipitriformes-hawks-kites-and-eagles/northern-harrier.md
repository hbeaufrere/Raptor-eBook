# Northern harrier

_Circus hudsonius_

## Description

### General Appearance

The Northern Harrier is a slim-bodied, medium-sized hawk with **long, narrow wings, a long tail, long slender legs, and a conspicuous white rump patch present in all ages and sexes**. The face appears distinctly owlish because of a **facial ruff (disc) similar in structure and function to that of owls**, which channels sound and allows the harrier to detect prey acoustically even without visual cues, a unique feature among North American diurnal raptors. The typical flight is low (under 5 m), buoyant, and quartering, with the wings held in a dihedral and the body teetering from side to side. When soaring high with wings pulled back it can suggest a large falcon, though the tail is proportionally much longer.

### Adult Plumage

**This is the only strongly sexually dichromatic hawk among common California raptors.** The adult male ("gray ghost") is pale gray above, mostly white below with variable rusty markings, with black wingtips (p6 to p10) and a black trailing edge to the secondaries. The adult female is brown above and buffy below with brown streaking, with barred rectrices and noticeably darker secondaries below. In both sexes the white uppertail coverts form the diagnostic rump patch. Some older males become progressively paler over successive molts.

### Juvenile Plumage

Juveniles resemble the adult female but are darker chocolate brown above and **strongly rufous below, especially in fall**, with heavy streaking limited mostly to the upper breast; the rufous wash bleaches to pale tan by late winter, making streaks more apparent. The underside of the secondaries is noticeably darker than in adult females.

### Differences Between Sexes

Females average about **50 percent heavier and 12.5 percent larger than males**. In addition to the adult plumage dichromatism, iris color separates the sexes remarkably early: **at 11 to 14 days of age, nestling males have gray-cast irides and females chocolate brown**, a criterion useful for sexing nestlings and juveniles in hand. Juvenile males show pale greenish-yellow to smoke-gray eyes, becoming lemon yellow over the second year; juvenile females keep brown eyes flecked with yellow, reaching mostly yellow only by the end of the third year. Females also have thicker, proportionally shorter tarsi.

### Bare Parts

The bill is dark, bluish basally in first-year birds, with a yellow cere (sometimes greenish-tinged) in adults. Legs and feet are pale to vivid orange-yellow, long and slender, adapted for striking prey through vegetation. Iris color varies by age and sex as described above.

### Subspecies

The Northern Harrier is now treated as a **monotypic** species, _Circus hudsonius_, split from the Old World Hen Harrier (_Circus cyaneus_). Geographic variation in appearance across North America is slight.

## Distribution and Migration

### Overall Range

The Northern Harrier breeds widely but locally across North America, from northern Alaska and Canada south to the northern Baja Peninsula, with the highest breeding densities in wet grasslands and marshes; the US and Canada population is estimated at roughly 790,000 individuals. It winters from southern Canada through the conterminous United States, Mexico, and Central America to Panama, with high winter densities in California, the Southwest, and Texas. Northern populations are migratory; migration is diurnal and mostly solitary, and, unlike buteos, harriers migrate readily on light winds and cross open water. The species is also markedly **nomadic, with local breeding densities tracking vole abundance** from year to year.

### California

In California the harrier is a **common winter visitor to open country statewide** (Christmas Bird Count data show notably high winter densities in parts of the state) and a **local breeder** in coastal marshes, the Central Valley, and northeastern plateau grasslands; it is absent or rare as a breeder in mountainous and desert regions. Breeding habitat is ground-nesting cover in wet meadows, freshwater and salt marshes, and ungrazed grassland, which makes nests highly vulnerable to mowing, agricultural operations, and predators. The breeding population has declined with the loss of wetlands and native grasslands, and the **Northern Harrier is a California Species of Special Concern as a breeding species**. Ground nesting also means that "grounded" fledglings found in fields are often normal wanderers rather than orphans, a useful triage consideration during spring hay-cutting season.

## Diet and Biology

### Diet

The harrier is an opportunistic open-country predator whose diet varies strongly by season and region. In summer it takes small and medium-sized mammals (chiefly voles and other rodents), birds (passerines and small waterbirds, including many recently fledged young), reptiles, and frogs. In winter in the northern part of the range it feeds almost exclusively on _Microtus_ voles (84 to 93 percent by frequency); in southern regions mammals such as cotton rats and house mice dominate, with a higher proportion of passerines. Mean vertebrate prey mass is about 50 g (range roughly 7 to 1,000 g). Harriers also scavenge carcasses, take wounded or sickly prey, and are known to subdue large prey by drowning. Breeding adults cache and retrieve surplus food.

### Foraging Behavior

The harrier **hunts almost entirely on the wing, coursing low over the ground with buoyant, gliding flight** interspersed with flapping, using transect flight, quartering, and border-following along ditches and edges, and finishing with hook, hover, or straight pounces. The facial disc allows acoustic prey location in dense cover. Capture success is highly variable (5 to 35 percent of pounces) and declines with prey agility (74 percent for amphibians and reptiles, 34 percent for small mammals, 14 percent for birds). Males hunt in more open habitat and take more birds; females hunt taller, denser vegetation. Small prey are swallowed whole; birds are plucked, usually on the ground. A captive female consumed about 100 g/day in fall and winter, roughly 19 percent of body mass.

### Reproduction

**The harrier is a ground nester and is regularly polygynous**, both exceptional traits among North American hawks. Males may pair with several females (harems) simultaneously; polygynous males rear substantially more offspring than monogamous males, while secondary females often receive less male provisioning and fledge fewer young. Courtship features the spectacular "sky-dance" of steep undulating dives and climbs. The nest is a platform of reeds, grasses, and sticks built on the ground in dense, often wet vegetation (nests over water are bulkier); the female does most construction over 7 to 14 days. Most eggs are laid mid-April to early June depending on latitude; the mean clutch is 4.4 (range about 3 to 6, up to 9 reported), laid at roughly 2-day intervals, and incubated by the female alone for 30 to 32 days while the male provides all her food, transferring prey in dramatic aerial food passes. Hatching is asynchronous, establishing a persistent size hierarchy. Nestlings become ambulatory at about 2 weeks and move into surrounding vegetation along narrow runways, an important point when searching for "missing" chicks at disturbed nests. First flight occurs at about 29 to 37 days (males earlier than females). Mean productivity is about 1.8 fledged young per nest. About 16 to 23 percent of breeding females are yearlings or subadults; yearling males breed only rarely.

## Molt

The Northern Harrier exhibits a **Modified Basic Strategy**, with incomplete-to-complete prebasic molts, a limited preformative molt in some individuals, and no prealternate molt. There are 10 functional primaries, 13 to 14 secondaries (including 3 tertials), and 12 rectrices.

The prejuvenile molt is complete in the nest (June to August), with juvenile feathers essentially fully grown except remiges and rectrices by 35 to 40 days. The preformative molt is absent in most individuals but can include up to 50 percent of body feathers (December to April) with no flight feathers.

The second prebasic molt (April to November) is incomplete to complete; about 5 to 11 percent of individuals retain 1 to 6 juvenile secondaries among s3 to s4 and s7 to s10. Females typically reach adult-like (definitive) plumage at the second basic plumage, whereas **males often pass through a browner, intermediate second basic plumage and reach definitive gray plumage at the third prebasic molt**.

The definitive prebasic molt (June to December) proceeds with primaries replaced distally (p1 to p10), secondaries from s1, s5, and the tertials, and rectrices usually in the sequence r1, r6, r3, r4, r2, r5. In breeding birds, molt can begin during incubation, suspend during chick-feeding, and resume after fledging; suspension is more frequent in females (about 46 percent) than males (about 25 percent). Stepwise (Staffelmauser) replacement occurs occasionally among the secondaries after incomplete molts.
