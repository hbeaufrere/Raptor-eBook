# Cooper's hawk

_Astur cooperii_

## Description

### General Appearance

The Cooper's Hawk is a crow-sized hawk with **short, rounded wings and a long, rounded tail**, a silhouette built for rapid, maneuverable pursuit of birds through dense vegetation. Recent taxonomic revisions moved the species from _Accipiter_ to the genus _Astur_, together with the American Goshawk. Total length averages approximately 39 cm in males and 45 cm in females in the eastern US; western birds, including those in California, are significantly smaller. In flight, the head projects far beyond the wrists (a key contrast with the Sharp-shinned Hawk), and the long tail is at least slightly graduated with a relatively wide white terminal band. In profile, the curve of the bill follows that of the forehead in a seamless interface, unlike the jutting bill of the Sharp-shinned Hawk. Birds of all ages frequently raise their hackles (long nape feathers), producing an almost triangular head shape and exposing extensive white feather bases.

### Adult Plumage

Adults have **blue-gray to grayish-brown upperparts with a dark crown contrasting sharply with a paler gray nape**, and white underparts with wide rufous barring. The undertail coverts are unmarked, fluffy white. **Males are more brightly colored than females**, with generally blue-gray upperparts, a charcoal to distinctly black crown, and gray cheeks; females are browner above with brown or reddish cheeks and a less contrasting crown. The tail is gray with 3 to 4 blackish transverse bars and is often extensively tipped white in fresh plumage.

### Juvenile Plumage

Juveniles are medium brown above with a variable number of large white patches near the bases of some scapulars and wing coverts giving a checkered look, and chestnut feather fringes that fade to whitish. The underparts are whitish to pale buff with **distinct, well-defined, narrow teardrop-shaped brown streaks** ending on the belly; the undertail coverts are typically unstreaked white. This clean, crisp ventral streaking distinguishes juveniles from the messier, redder, more barred streaking of most juvenile Sharp-shinned Hawks and from the blobby black undertail streaks of juvenile American Goshawks. The tail shows about 6 broad dusky bars running straight across (a zigzag pattern in the goshawk) with narrow white tips.

### Differences Between Sexes

**The female is about one-third larger than the male, among the greatest reversed sexual size dimorphism of any accipitrid in the world.** Plumage is similar between sexes in juveniles and differs somewhat in adults (males brighter, bluer, with darker crowns and gray cheeks). Body mass separates the sexes reliably: in western North America breeding males average approximately 280 to 300 g and females approximately 475 to 525 g. Sexes become recognizably different in tarsal width from about day 12 in the nest.

### Bare Parts

The bill is gray to blue-gray basally and blackish distally, with a dull to pale yellow or greenish-yellow cere. Legs and feet are dull to bright yellow with black talons; the legs are thicker than the pencil-thin tarsi of the Sharp-shinned Hawk. **Iris color changes with age: grayish-white to pale yellow in young nestlings, clear bright yellow in juveniles, then progressively orange to deep red in older adults**, with males averaging darker and redder than females of the same age. Detectable change ceases at about 5 years of age, and variability within age groups makes eye color alone insufficient for aging, though it is useful combined with molt and plumage criteria.

### Subspecies

The Cooper's Hawk is **monotypic**. Geographic variation is limited to size: eastern birds are larger (by roughly 20 percent in mass) than western birds, an important consideration when weighing California patients against reference values from eastern literature.

## Distribution and Migration

### Overall Range

The Cooper's Hawk breeds throughout the conterminous United States, southern Canada, and at middle and higher elevations in Mexico west of the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, primarily south of the boreal forest in deciduous and mixed forests. Over recent decades the range and numbers have expanded greatly, particularly in urban areas; **since the 1970s the species has colonized suburban and urban landscapes to the point that it is likely the most common backyard breeding raptor in North America**. The US and Canada population was estimated at approximately 800,000 individuals.

Birds from roughly the northern third of the breeding range are migratory, moving south in autumn singly or in small groups along ridges and coastlines; most individuals winter within the conterminous United States and Mexico, less frequently south to Costa Rica. Many birds winter close to their breeding sites, and migratory tendency varies regionally and individually. Notably, some birds breeding in southern California have been documented migrating northward, up to nearly 1,000 km. Fall migration runs from late August to early November with a peak from late September to mid-October; spring migration occurs March through May, with males preceding females and adults preceding yearlings.

### California

The Cooper's Hawk is a **widespread year-round resident throughout California**, breeding in oak woodlands, riparian forest, conifer stands, and increasingly in suburban and urban neighborhoods (the first breeding record for San Francisco dates to 1994). Historical declines in the early and mid-1900s included the loss of breeders from lowland riparian areas of southern California, especially the Colorado River valley, where riparian woodland was greatly reduced. Populations have since recovered strongly; Breeding Bird Survey data show an increasing trend in California of about 1.2 percent per year since 1966. Oaks are the favored nest trees in California (75 of 77 nests in one study). Urban and suburban birds hunting songbirds and doves around backyard feeders are a frequent source of admissions to rehabilitation centers, most often after window collisions and vehicular trauma.

## Diet and Biology

### Diet

The Cooper's Hawk is primarily a **bird specialist**: most prey are live, ground- and shrub-foraging small to medium songbirds and doves, secondarily small mammals (chipmunks, tree and ground squirrels, mice) and lizards. Of 12 breeding-season diet studies across North America, 10 reported birds as the dominant prey group, ranging from 56 to 96 percent of items. The most frequent prey species include Mourning Dove and other doves, European Starling, American Robin, jays, Northern Flicker, and House Sparrow; in central and western states, mammals make up a higher proportion of the diet. Prey mass is typically about 30 to 130 g. Nestling and fledgling birds made up nearly two-thirds of known-age prey in one Florida study. A high proportion of doves in the diet of urban birds is linked to a high incidence of trichomoniasis: infection rates of 85 percent were reported in urban Arizona nestlings, where the disease caused half of urban nestling deaths, a point of direct clinical relevance when handling this species.

### Foraging Behavior

Hunting typically relies on concealment: a series of brief perch-and-scan episodes followed by a sudden burst of speed, or low, shielded flight using bushes and buildings to conceal an oblique approach, ending in a rapid strike with the feet swung forward. Surprise attacks using physical obstructions are more successful than open-field pursuits. The species also stoops from higher flight, occasionally pursues prey on foot, is known to drown prey by holding it underwater, and has been documented hunting roosting birds at night under urban lighting. Reported capture success rates range from about 20 percent (urban adults) to over 50 percent (groups of fledglings). Both sexes cache uneaten prey on horizontal branches during breeding. A captive male consumed an average of 63 g/day and a female 70 g/day, roughly 16 to 20 percent of body mass.

### Reproduction

Pairs form in early spring (both sexes are present near nests by early March, and urban birds may remain near territories year-round). Recent genetic work shows high rates of extra-pair paternity involving territorial birds and non-territorial floaters. The male does most of the nest building (about 70 percent), typically high in a live tree, often on a pre-existing squirrel or hawk nest base; construction takes about two weeks. Clutches of typically 3 to 5 pale cobalt-blue eggs (mean 3.3 to 4.4; range 1 to 8) are laid at 2-day intervals from early April (California and other southern populations) to mid-May. Incubation, 30 to 36 days, is almost entirely by the female, with the male delivering prey to her at a transfer perch about 3 times daily. Hatching is asynchronous over 1 to 3 days. The female alone broods and feeds the nestlings bill to bill until they can dismember prey at 18 to 21 days. Young fledge at about 25 to 34 days (males before females), return to the nest for food deliveries for at least 10 days, and are fed by the parents (particularly the male) for up to 7 weeks post-fledging. Age at first breeding is typically 2 years; yearling females breed regularly (16 to 25 percent of breeding females in some urban studies), whereas breeding by yearling males is rare. There is a single brood per season, with replacement clutches after early loss.

## Molt

The Cooper's Hawk exhibits a **Modified Basic Strategy**, with incomplete-to-complete prebasic molts, a limited preformative molt in some individuals, and no prealternate molt. The species has 10 primaries, 13 secondaries (including 3 tertials), and 12 rectrices.

The prejuvenile molt is complete, June to August, in the nest; juvenile feathering is nearly complete by day 28, though remiges and rectrices do not reach full length until about days 45 to 54.

The preformative molt is absent to limited (absent in about one-third of individuals), September to April on the nonbreeding grounds, involving up to 10 percent of body feathers but no flight feathers. Replaced formative feathers on the upper breast can appear contrastingly fresh and buffy with broader streaks.

The second prebasic molt is incomplete to complete, primarily April to August, and resembles the definitive molt in sequence but starts earlier in the year because most yearlings are not breeding. About 30 percent of individuals retain 1 to 6 juvenile secondaries (among s3 to s4 and s7 to s10), and occasionally juvenile rectrices or coverts; roughly half of birds are indistinguishable from older adults after a complete second prebasic molt.

The definitive prebasic molt is incomplete to complete, primarily May to October, on or near the breeding grounds. Primaries are replaced distally (p1 to p10), secondaries proximally from s1 and s5 and distally from the tertials, and rectrices usually in the sequence r1, r3, r4, r6, r5, r2. **In breeding birds, molt commonly begins during incubation, suspends during chick-feeding, and resumes after fledging**; suspension occurs in more females (about 62 percent) than males (about 29 percent), and the resulting "suspension limits" (contrasts among feather generations) can be identified in the fall wing. One to six basic secondaries are also retained in 30 to 40 percent of individuals after each definitive molt, so mixed feather generations in the wing are normal in adults.
