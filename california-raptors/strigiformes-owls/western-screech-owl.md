# Western screech owl

_Megascops kennicottii_

## Description

### General Appearance

The Western Screech-Owl is a **small (19 to 25.5 cm), stocky, cryptically patterned owl with short feathered ear tufts and lemon-yellow eyes**. Plumage is gray to gray-brown, streaked and finely barred, providing superb bark-like camouflage against tree trunks; the pale facial disc has a dark lateral border. The wings are rounded and the tail short and squared. The song, a series of 5 to 15 hollow whistled hoots accelerating like a bouncing ball, is unique and is the easiest way to separate it from the Eastern Screech-Owl (which gives a descending whinny). The much smaller Flammulated Owl has dark brown eyes.

### Adult Plumage

Adults have gray-brown upperparts with dark central shaft streaks and fine crossbars, buff-spotted scapulars forming a pale line down the side of the back, and whitish underparts with **heavy dark central streaks crossed by fine perpendicular bars**. Unlike the Eastern Screech-Owl, true rufous morphs are essentially absent, though about 7 percent of Pacific Northwest coastal birds are quite reddish-brown. There is no seasonal variation and sexes are alike.

### Juvenile Plumage

Nestlings hatch in creamy white down replaced by grayish, sepia-barred down. Juvenile body plumage is loosely textured with fine, even dusky and whitish barring on the crown, back, and underparts (lacking the bold streaks of adults), and is replaced by adult-like formative plumage over the first fall. First-year birds retain all juvenile flight feathers; the outer primaries are thinner, more tapered, and more heavily buff-spotted than in adults, though formative and adult plumages can be difficult to distinguish.

### Differences Between Sexes

Plumage is identical; females average larger (females 120 to 305 g, males 100 to 230 g), but **sexual size dimorphism is less marked than in most North American owls**, likely related to the largely invertebrate diet in southern populations. In Idaho birds, female mass peaks at about 255 g just before laying. Body mass in both sexes peaks in early winter and is lowest in late spring.

### Bare Parts

The bill is blackish to dark gray in most of the range (yellowish-green in the Eastern Screech-Owl, a helpful in-hand check), the iris pale to lemon yellow, the toes bristled to feathered (feathered in northern populations, bristled in desert birds), and the claws yellow-olive to gray, darker at the tips.

### Subspecies

Up to nine subspecies occur in western North America and Mexico, varying in size, ground color, and foot feathering; California hosts coastal and interior forms, with grayer birds in the deserts.

## Distribution and Migration

### Overall Range

The Western Screech-Owl is a **strictly resident** owl of wooded lowlands of western North America, from coastal southern Alaska and British Columbia south through the western United States to Baja California and the interior highlands of Mexico. It is a bird of low elevations: riparian woodland, oak woodland, desert washes with saguaros, and suburban parks and gardens, generally avoiding higher mountains and dense wet forest. The species has been expanding slowly eastward along riparian corridors on the Great Plains. Juveniles disperse from natal territories about two months after fledging, but adults are highly sedentary.

### California

The species occurs **throughout most of California at lower elevations**: coastal and foothill oak woodland, riparian forest, and desert riparian areas, but is absent from the higher mountains, much of the Mojave Desert, and the Salton Sea Basin, and it has disappeared from some heavily urbanized coastal lowlands of southern California. In oak and riparian habitat it is often the most abundant raptor of any kind, and its dependence on cavities ties it to mature trees, especially oaks, cottonwoods, and sycamores with flicker holes and natural hollows. Typical admissions at California centers include vehicle strikes (it hunts low along roads at night), window collisions, cavity-tree removal during spring tree work (whole broods), entanglement, and cat attacks in suburban areas.

## Diet and Biology

### Diet

The Western Screech-Owl takes a remarkably **broad diet of small animals: small mammals (mostly 16 to 35 g, mice, voles, woodrats, kangaroo rats), small birds (House Sparrows are a frequent target), earthworms, insects (Jerusalem crickets are a staple in California and the Southwest), crayfish, fish, and treefrogs**. It is more insectivorous than its eastern counterpart, with desert populations taking the highest proportion of invertebrates. It is capable of killing prey up to the size of a cottontail or Mallard, far above its own mass. Mean prey-to-predator mass ratio was about 9 percent in Idaho. In winter it caches beheaded mice and small birds in cavities.

### Foraging Behavior

A nocturnal **sit-and-wait predator**, leaving its roost around sunset and hunting from inconspicuous perches beneath the canopy with a clear view of the ground. Most prey are taken on the ground, but it also gleans arthropods from foliage, hawks moths and even bats in midair, snatches crayfish from shallow water, and gathers earthworms from road surfaces after rain. Adults will hunt by day when feeding large broods. Captive intake was about 51 g dry mass per kg body mass per day, with evidence of a relatively low metabolic rate.

### Reproduction

Courtship begins in January and February, with the male singing at the nest site and courtship-feeding the female. This is an obligate **cavity nester that adds no material**: nests are old flicker or Pileated Woodpecker holes, natural hollows in oaks, cottonwoods, sycamores, and bigleaf maples, saguaro cavities in the desert, and nest boxes, typically 1 to 12 m up, with entrances close to the owl's own body diameter. Sites are reused for years. Eggs are laid from mid-March to mid-April in most of the range (as early as late February). The clutch is typically 3 to 5 white eggs (range 2 to 7, larger inland than on the coast). **Only the female incubates, about 26 to 34 days**, fed entirely by the male, who roosts progressively closer to the nest, often within 5 m by hatching. Hatchlings weigh about 14 g; young fledge at roughly 4 to 5 weeks at about 75 percent of adult mass, leaving the cavity at night as an entire brood and clambering foot-over-foot up branches when grounded, a behavior worth remembering before "rescuing" a fledgling at the base of a tree. The family stays together about 5 weeks, and juveniles disperse from late June to early August. Single brood, with replacement clutches after early failure.

## Molt

The Western Screech-Owl exhibits a **Complex Basic Strategy** with a partial preformative molt, complete (occasionally incomplete) definitive prebasic molts, and no prealternate molt. There are 10 primaries, 12 to 14 secondaries (including 3 tertials), and 12 rectrices.

The prejuvenile molt is completed in the nest; primaries and rectrices are only about half grown at fledging (around 28 days), so recent fledglings are poor fliers by design. The preformative molt (July to November) replaces most or all body feathers and some lesser coverts but **no flight feathers**. The definitive prebasic molt (June to October, after breeding) is usually complete; small owls, unlike large ones, can renew the whole wing in one season, and the rectrices may be dropped nearly synchronously, so a briefly nearly tailless owl in late summer is normal. Occasionally up to 5 secondaries (among s3 to s4 and s7 to s9) are retained, allowing some birds to be aged beyond their second year.
