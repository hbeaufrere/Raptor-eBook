# Burrowing owl

_Athene cunicularia_

## Description

### General Appearance

The Burrowing Owl is a **small (19 to 25 cm, roughly 120 to 230 g), long-legged, ground-dwelling owl active by day**, which makes it nearly unmistakable. The round head lacks ear tufts, the facial ruff is oval with a broad buffy-white eyebrow-to-malar frame, the eyes are lemon yellow, and the sparsely feathered legs are strikingly long, an adaptation to a terrestrial life in and around mammal burrows. Its habit of standing upright on burrow mounds or fence posts and **bobbing up and down when disturbed** is characteristic.

### Adult Plumage

Adults are brown above, profusely spotted with buffy white on the crown, back, and scapulars, with a white throat separated from the barred underparts by a mottled brown chest band; the breast, belly, and flanks are broadly barred brown on a whitish ground. The wings and short tail are barred brown and buffy white.

### Juvenile Plumage

Juveniles differ obviously from adults in their **solid buff, unbarred chest and belly and a buffy patch across the upperwing coverts**. They molt into adult-like formative plumage rapidly and are indistinguishable from adults by about 70 days after fledging apart from retained juvenile greater coverts and flight feathers detectable in the hand.

### Differences Between Sexes

Uniquely among familiar owls, **reversed sexual size dimorphism is essentially absent**: males are actually slightly larger in linear measurements, while females are somewhat heavier. Plumage is similar, but by late spring **males are noticeably paler than females from sun-bleaching**, because the male stands guard outside while the female spends her time in the burrow. During breeding, behavior is the best field criterion for sex.

### Bare Parts

The bill is cream to greenish-yellow, the cere gray, and the iris lemon yellow (a small percentage of birds, mostly in Florida, have brown irides). The leg and toe feathering is white to beige and bristle-like; the exposed skin is dark gray.

### Subspecies

Numerous subspecies range from Canada to Tierra del Fuego and the Caribbean. All California birds belong to the Western Burrowing Owl, _A. c. hypugaea_; the Florida Burrowing Owl (_A. c. floridana_) is the other North American form.

## Distribution and Migration

### Overall Range

The Western Burrowing Owl breeds in open grasslands, steppes, deserts, and agricultural lands of western North America from southern Canada into Mexico, with the species as a whole extending through Central and South America and the Caribbean. Northern Great Plains and Great Basin populations are migratory, wintering in the southwestern United States and Mexico, while southern populations, including most in California, are resident; migrants from the north also winter in California, including on the Channel Islands. The North American range has **contracted southward and westward**, with extirpations and steep declines across the northern prairies.

### California

Burrowing owls occur in California's grasslands, deserts, and agricultural landscapes, with **strongholds in the Imperial Valley (one of the densest populations anywhere) and parts of the Central Valley**, and severe declines or local extirpation in coastal southern California and much of the San Francisco Bay Area as grasslands are developed. The species is closely tied to **California ground squirrel colonies**, whose burrows provide nearly all natural nest sites; squirrel eradication removes owl habitat as effectively as bulldozing does. The Burrowing Owl is a **California Species of Special Concern**, and mitigation-driven passive relocation and artificial burrow programs are common in the state. Frequent admission causes include vehicle strikes (they forage low along roads at dusk), burrow destruction during grading and discing, entanglement, and predation injuries; rehabilitation and release planning must account for their colonial tendencies and burrow dependence.

## Diet and Biology

### Diet

The Burrowing Owl is an opportunistic generalist. **Invertebrates (grasshoppers, crickets, beetles, moths, earwigs, scorpions) dominate the diet numerically (often about 90 percent of items), but small vertebrates, mainly mice and voles, supply 70 to 95 percent of the biomass.** In the Imperial Valley, orthopterans alone made up 59 percent of stomach items. Vertebrate prey extends to small birds, reptiles, amphibians, bats, and young ground squirrels. Large **prey caches** are accumulated in the nest burrow before and during laying, sometimes spectacular (a cache of 210 voles and 2 deer mice was recorded in Saskatchewan). Pellets are egested daily.

### Foraging Behavior

Activity spans the full 24 hours: video studies show that **insects are captured mostly by day, largely by the female near the nest, while vertebrates are taken at dawn and dusk, mostly by the male**, who typically hunts within 1 km of the nest. Hunting techniques include walking and hopping after insects on the ground, flycatching from a perch or mound, hovering, and perch-hunting for rodents. Prey is caught with the feet and transferred to the bill. Physiologically the species is notable for a high tolerance of carbon dioxide (an adaptation to burrow life) and for using the burrow microclimate to buffer temperature extremes.

### Reproduction

Nesting is **underground, in burrows dug by ground squirrels, prairie dogs, badgers, and other mammals**; California birds rarely dig their own (unlike Florida birds), underscoring their dependence on burrowing mammals. Both sexes maintain the burrow and famously line the entrance and chamber with dried livestock dung, which attracts insect prey and signals occupancy. Nest areas typically contain many satellite burrows used for escape and roosting. Laying occurs from March to May (from the third week of March in the Southwest; late April in the northern range). The clutch is large, typically 7 to 9 white eggs (range about 4 to 12), laid at roughly 36 hour intervals. **Incubation is 28 to 30 days by the female only**, with the male providing all food; hatching is asynchronous. Hatchlings weigh about 9 g. Young emerge from the burrow at about 2 weeks, run and hop at 3 weeks, make short flights at 4 weeks, and fledge at 44 to 53 days, practicing prey capture on insects delivered by the parents. Broods of juveniles standing at burrow mouths are vulnerable to predation, vehicles, and agricultural machinery in their first two weeks above ground. One brood per season is the rule, with occasional documented double brooding in California and Florida and renesting after early failure.

## Molt

The Burrowing Owl exhibits a **Complex Basic Strategy**: a complete prejuvenile molt in the burrow, a partial preformative molt, complete definitive prebasic molts, and no confirmed prealternate molt. There are 10 primaries, only 9 secondaries (including 3 tertials), and 12 rectrices.

Juvenile plumage appears complete by 30 to 35 days, though the remiges and rectrices reach full length only around 50 days. The rapid **preformative molt begins as early as 45 to 70 days of age** and replaces most body plumage (producing the adult-like barred underparts) plus lesser and median coverts, but no flight feathers; retained juvenile greater coverts and slightly narrower rectrices identify first-year birds in the hand. The definitive prebasic molt is **complete in a single season (June to September)**, beginning around fledging of the young: primaries proceed from a molt center around p3 or p4 (or from p1), secondaries from s1, s5, and the tertials, and the rectrices may be shed nearly simultaneously, so a briefly tail-less bird in late summer is normal, not pathological.
