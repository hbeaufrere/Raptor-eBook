# Spotted owl

_Strix occidentalis_

## Description

### General Appearance

The Spotted Owl is a **medium-sized (about 47 to 48 cm), round-headed, dark-eyed brown owl without ear tufts**, chocolate to chestnut brown with round to elliptical white spots on the head, neck, back, and underparts. The large brownish facial disc shows indistinct darker concentric rings around each eye, and the dark brown eyes appear almost black, separating it at a glance from every other similar-sized California owl except the Barred Owl. The legs and feet are fully feathered. It is famously **tame and approachable**, often allowing observers within a few meters, and moves through the forest with short gliding flights.

### Adult Plumage

Adults show white spotting throughout: small spots on the crown and nape and larger elliptical spots (actually interrupted white bars) on the chest, abdomen, flanks, and wings. The **Barred Owl**, its critical confusion species, is slightly larger and paler with horizontal barring on the breast and vertical streaking on the belly rather than spots. **Barred x Spotted Owl hybrids ("sparred owls")** look like large, pale Spotted Owls with intermediate patterning and are increasingly encountered in the Northern Spotted Owl's range.

### Juvenile Plumage

Nestlings hatch in white down, replaced from about 10 days by light brown, dark-barred mesoptile juvenile plumage. Fledglings retain downy tufts on the tail tips. A useful age criterion in the field and hand: **juvenile rectrices with pointed, pure white tips are retained for about 26 months**, so first-year and second-year "subadults" can be distinguished from adults, whose rectrix tips are rounded and mottled.

### Differences Between Sexes

Plumage is alike, with the female larger than the male: for the California subspecies, males 470 to 685 g and females 535 to 775 g; Northern birds are slightly heavier (females to 850 g) and Mexican birds smaller. Voice differs, with the male's four-note location call lower pitched.

### Bare Parts

The bill is yellowish green (gray in fledglings), the iris dark brown, and the legs and feet fully feathered. Dark eyes in a nocturnal owl are unusual and shared in California only with the Barn Owl and Barred Owl.

### Subspecies

Three subspecies are recognized: the **Northern Spotted Owl (_S. o. caurina_)**, from British Columbia south through coastal California to Marin County; the **California Spotted Owl (_S. o. occidentalis_)**, in the Sierra Nevada, southern Cascades, and coastal and Transverse Ranges from Monterey County to Baja California; and the **Mexican Spotted Owl (_S. o. lucida_)** of the southwestern US and Mexico. California hosts the first two, which differ subtly (Northern birds darker with smaller spots).

## Distribution and Migration

### Overall Range

The Spotted Owl is resident in forested western North America from southwestern British Columbia to central Mexico, from sea level to about 1,200 m in the north and 2,700 m in the Southwest. It is tied to **mature, multilayered, high-canopy forest**, and its association with old growth made the Northern Spotted Owl the emblem of Pacific Northwest forest conservation. There is no true migration, but some individuals, especially California Spotted Owls in the Sierra Nevada, make **altitudinal movements in winter** to lower-elevation foothill riparian and hardwood forests.

### California

Both California subspecies face serious conservation pressure. The **Northern Spotted Owl is federally listed as Threatened and is also listed as Threatened under the California Endangered Species Act**; the California Spotted Owl is a state Species of Special Concern and has been proposed for federal listing. The threats are habitat loss from logging and high-severity wildfire, and, increasingly, **competition and hybridization with the invading Barred Owl**, which displaces Spotted Owls from territories and is the subject of removal programs. **Anticoagulant rodenticides associated with illegal cannabis cultivation on forest lands** have emerged as an additional documented mortality source in California Spotted and Northern Spotted Owls. Any Spotted Owl presented for rehabilitation is a high-stakes patient: agencies (USFWS and CDFW) should be contacted early, and every individual matters demographically.

## Diet and Biology

### Diet

The Spotted Owl feeds mainly on **small and medium-sized nocturnal mammals, with two staples: the northern flying squirrel in northern and higher-elevation conifer forest, and the dusky-footed woodrat in lower-elevation and coastal woodland**. Home ranges are markedly smaller where woodrats, the larger prey, dominate. Other prey include deer mice, red tree voles, red-backed voles, gophers, moles, rabbits and hares, diurnal squirrels, small birds (including smaller owls), bats, amphibians, and insects (numerous but a trivial share of biomass). Surplus prey is cached on moss-covered limbs, stumps, or the ground, often in cool microsites, and owls may roost next to their caches.

### Foraging Behavior

Almost entirely a nocturnal **perch-and-pounce predator**, foraging from about an hour before sunset to just before sunrise in mature forest, taking prey from the ground and from trunks and limbs; it occasionally hawks insects and bats on the wing and opportunistically takes prey by day at the roost. It kills by severing the cervical vertebrae with the bill and often mantles prey on the ground. Thermal physiology is notable: the species has a **narrow thermoneutral zone and is prone to heat stress**, thermoregulating behaviorally by roosting in shaded, multilayered canopy (one hypothesis for its old-forest association), which is also directly relevant to housing this species in captivity during California summers.

### Reproduction

Spotted Owls are long-lived, slowly reproducing residents with strong site fidelity; pairs occupy the same territories for years and begin roosting together and duetting by February and March. Like other owls they **build no nest**, using broken-top trees, cavities, platforms (old raptor nests, squirrel nests, mistletoe brooms), and occasionally cliff holes; cavity nests predominate in the Sierra Nevada. Clutches are initiated from early March to mid-April, typically **2 eggs (range 1 to 4)**, laid at about 72 hour intervals and incubated by the female alone for about 30 days while the male provisions her. The female broods continuously for the first 8 to 10 days. Young leave the nest at only **34 to 36 days, before they can fly well**; within 3 days they climb and glide into neighboring trees, and grounded, clambering fledglings in May and June are usually normal (parents continue care nearby, and misplaced "rescues" should be avoided). Both parents feed the fledglings through August; juveniles disperse in September and October. Crucially, **most pairs do not nest every year**, renesting after failure is rare, and there is never a second brood, so annual productivity is very low, one reason population recovery is so slow.

## Molt

Molt in this species is protracted and distinctive. The prejuvenile molt finishes contour feathers by about 34 to 36 days, remiges by 65 days, and rectrices by 75 days. The **first prebasic (Basic I) molt, starting at 8 to 9 weeks, is partial and replaces no remiges or rectrices**: juvenile flight feathers, including the white, pointed-tipped rectrices, are kept for about 26 months, defining the first-year and second-year subadult classes.

Adults molt from April through October. The definitive prebasic molt of remiges is **incomplete and gradual ("wrap-around" molt): only part of the primary and secondary series is replaced each year, with the remainder the following year**, and occasionally primaries are kept more than 2 years, so adults always carry mixed feather generations. Primaries p1 to p3 molt in variable order, then p4 to p10 proceed from outer to inner. Rectrix molt is variable: most often rapid and essentially complete in one season, sometimes split within a season or across two years, with all rectrices replaced at least every 2 years.
