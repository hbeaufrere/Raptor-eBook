# American barn owl

_Tyto furcata_

## Description

### General Appearance

The American Barn Owl is a medium-sized owl (males 32 to 39 cm, females 33 to 40 cm in North America) with a **big, round head without ear tufts and a complete, conspicuous heart-shaped facial ruff**. The eyes are dark and relatively small for an owl. The wings are long and rounded, flight is buoyant and completely silent (to human ears and at ultrasonic frequencies), and the **long, sparsely feathered legs extend past the tail in flight**, an adaptation for plunging feet-first into vegetation or snow. The middle claw bears a pectinate (comb-like) inner edge used in preening, a Tytonidae trait absent in other California owls. Recent taxonomy splits the New World birds as _Tyto furcata_, the American Barn Owl, from the Old World Western Barn Owl (_Tyto alba_); the North American subspecies is _T. f. pratincola_, the largest barn owl taxon.

### Adult Plumage

Upperparts are golden-buff to ochraceous, finely vermiculated with gray and white and marked with small black and white spots; the underparts vary from pure white to dark buff with black spotting. No other California owl is this pale below.

### Juvenile Plumage

Nestlings wear dense white down followed by a filamentous grayish-white to buff second coat; pennaceous juvenile plumage is essentially adult-like, and wisps of down may persist on the head and underparts for 1 to 3 months after fledging. In the hand, first-year birds show uniformly narrow, tapered, faded flight feathers with narrower and more numerous dark bars, without the molt contrasts of adults.

### Differences Between Sexes

Females are significantly larger than males (males about 400 to 560 g, females 420 to 700 g) and **typically darker and more heavily spotted below**; some males are immaculate white underneath. Because the sexes overlap in plumage, the combination of breast ground color, spotting, and body mass sexes about 97 percent of birds correctly. In barn owls, female breast spotting correlates with offspring parasite resistance.

### Bare Parts

The bill is ivory to dull white with a pinkish-white cere usually hidden by feathers. The iris is dark brown to black at all ages. The lower tarsi and toes are gray in adults (pinkish to pale yellow in young birds), with dusky-gray talons. The **width and serration of the flange on the middle talon increase with age through at least the third year**, a useful ageing aid in the hand.

### Subspecies

Numerous subspecies occur across the Americas; all North American birds, including California's, are _T. f. pratincola_, nearly twice the mass of the smallest island forms.

## Distribution and Migration

### Overall Range

The American Barn Owl occurs from southern Canada through the United States, Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean to the southern tip of South America, avoiding mountainous and heavily forested regions and the coldest northern latitudes (it is poorly adapted to cold and requires thermally protective roosts in harsh winters). The species is essentially **resident**, with juveniles dispersing in random compass directions in their first fall; only the northernmost populations are partly migratory. It readily colonizes human structures and expanded in several regions where irrigation, forest clearing, or nest box programs created habitat.

### California

The barn owl **occurs throughout California in lowlands and lightly forested foothills**, from coastal marshes and the Central Valley (where it is common in agricultural landscapes) to the lower Colorado River valley, where it is fairly common. It nests in tree hollows, cliff crevices, arroyo banks, palm trees, barns, bridges, and especially nest boxes, which are widely deployed in California vineyards and orchards for rodent control. This close association with agriculture and roadsides has clinical consequences: **barn owls are among the most frequently admitted raptors in California, mostly from vehicle collisions along highways, and anticoagulant rodenticide exposure is a persistent concern** given their rodent-based diet and use of farm buildings.

## Diet and Biology

### Diet

The barn owl is a **small-mammal specialist: mammals made up 74 to 100 percent of prey in 52 North American diet studies**, mainly voles, mice, rats, gophers, and shrews, with pocket mice important in arid regions. In one Utah study of over 111,000 prey items, mammals were 98.2 percent of the diet. Birds (mostly communally roosting passerines such as starlings and blackbirds) are a minor component, and other taxa are rare. Its pellets preserve prey remains so well that barn owl pellet analysis underpins much of North American small-mammal distribution data. Wild birds consume roughly 110 to 150 g per day (about 2 to 3 voles); a captive female maintained mass on about 60 g per day. About two pellets are cast per day.

### Foraging Behavior

Hunting is almost entirely nocturnal, beginning about an hour after sunset. The owl quarters slowly and silently 1.5 to 4.5 m above open fields, grasslands, marshes, and desert scrub, or hunts from perches in denser cover, dropping onto prey with talons extended. Prey is located by exceptional low-light vision and, above all, by hearing: the barn owl's **ability to localize prey by sound alone is the most accurate of any animal tested**, allowing capture of prey completely hidden by vegetation or snow. Prey is usually killed with a bite through the back of the skull and swallowed whole. Surplus prey, sometimes 30 to 50 items and up to nearly 200, is stockpiled at the nest during incubation and early brooding.

### Reproduction

Barn owls are cavity nesters that build no nest; the female arranges a cup of her own shredded pellets. Pairs may roost together at the future nest site from November, and laying begins remarkably early (most California clutches begin in February; eggs have been recorded in every month in some regions). The white eggs are laid at 2 to 3 day intervals, and **incubation (29 to 34 days, mean about 31) begins with the first egg**, producing strongly asynchronous broods with a pronounced size hierarchy. Clutches are large for a raptor, commonly around 5 eggs and up to 10 or more, and **pairs regularly produce second broods in good prey years** (11 percent of pairs in Utah, over half in one California study), with three broods occasionally recorded; reproduction tracks rodent abundance closely. Only the female incubates and broods; the male delivers prey. Hatchlings weigh about 16 g, peak above adult mass around day 40, and fledge at about 55 to 64 days, returning to the nest cavity to roost for weeks; they are independent by roughly 11 to 12 weeks. Nestlings sleep lying down (a normal posture easily mistaken for weakness in the clinic) and defend themselves by rolling onto their backs and striking with the feet. This is a boom-and-bust species: productivity is high, but so are starvation losses, and most wild birds live only a few years.

## Molt

The American Barn Owl shows a Simple or Complex Basic Strategy with no prealternate molt; it has 10 primaries, 14 secondaries (including 3 tertials), and 12 rectrices. The prejuvenile molt is completed in and around the nest, with flight feather growth essentially complete at fledging (50 to 70 days).

Adult flight-feather molt differs from that of diurnal raptors: the **second prebasic molt (first summer to fall as a 1 year old) is incomplete, typically replacing only 1 to 3 primaries centered on p6** (the nodal feather in _Tyto_), 2 to 8 secondaries beginning with the tertials, and often most or all rectrices. Subsequent molts continue in sequence bidirectionally from p6 and distally from p1, so **juvenile primaries among p1 to p4 and p9 to p10 are commonly retained into the third and even fourth basic plumages**, giving reliable age classes in the hand: uniform juvenile remiges (first year), fresh mid-primaries against worn juvenile outer and inner primaries (second year), and mixed basic generations with or without a few residual juvenile feathers (third year and older). Molt occurs mainly from spring through fall in North America and overlaps breeding in females.
