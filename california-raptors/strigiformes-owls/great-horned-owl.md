# Great horned owl

_Bubo virginianus_

## Description

### General Appearance

The Great Horned Owl is the **only large (46 to 63 cm, 0.9 to 2.5 kg) North American owl with ear tufts, a white throat bib, and enormous yellow eyes**. It is a bulky, barrel-shaped owl with a moderately defined facial disc, mottled brown, buff, and black plumage, and long, broad wings held nearly horizontal in gliding flight. The deep, low-pitched multinote hoot is unmistakable. Confusion species are limited: the Long-eared Owl is much smaller and slimmer with closer-set tufts, and the Barred Owl lacks tufts and has dark eyes.

### Adult Plumage

Adults have tawny to ochraceous base coloration with sooty-brown mottled upperparts, a conspicuous crescentic white bib across the foreneck, underparts finely barred with brownish black over a pale buff to tawny ground, prominent black-edged ear tufts, and a tawny facial disc rimmed with black. The legs and toes are densely feathered to the talons. Sexes are alike in plumage at all ages, and there is no seasonal variation.

### Juvenile Plumage

Nestlings hatch in white down, replaced by day 8 with a grayish-buff mesoptile coat; juvenile body feathers are soft, loose, and downy with dusky barring, and ear tufts are only faintly indicated at fledging. Formative (first-year) plumage is essentially adult-like; **first-year birds are best recognized in the hand by uniform flight feathers of a single generation**, narrower and more tapered rectrices, and more distinct, closely spaced dark bars on the remiges.

### Differences Between Sexes

Females are larger and heavier than males (all subspecies combined, males average about 1,300 g and females about 1,700 g), but plumage is identical. In Iowa birds, a flattened wing chord of 370 mm separated 95 percent of males (below) from females (above). Skull width is the only skeletal measurement larger in males.

### Bare Parts

The bill and gape are slate black, largely hidden by bristly feathers. **The iris is brilliant lemon-yellow in adults** (steel gray in the first 2 to 3 weeks of life, fully yellow by 30 days). Talons are slate gray to black, and the grip is formidable: a force of about 13 kg is needed to open the closed foot, a point of real practical importance for handlers.

### Subspecies

Up to 15 subspecies are recognized from the subarctic to South America, varying moderately in size and color. California birds are mostly the relatively small, dusky Pacific subspecies (_B. v. pacificus_), with the paler desert form (_B. v. pallescens_) in the southeastern deserts.

## Distribution and Migration

### Overall Range

The Great Horned Owl has one of the largest ranges of any American bird, from the subarctic tree line through virtually all of North America to southern South America, occupying forests, deserts, grasslands with scattered trees, agricultural land, and suburbs. It is **nonmigratory; pairs typically remain on the same territory year-round for life**. In the boreal forest, numbers and movements track the 10-year snowshoe hare cycle, but temperate populations, including California's, are highly sedentary.

### California

The Great Horned Owl is a **common resident essentially throughout California**, from coastal lowlands and oak savanna through the Central Valley to deserts and mid-elevation conifer forest. It is one of the most frequently admitted owls at California rehabilitation facilities, typically for vehicle collisions, entanglement (fences, soccer nets, fishing line), electrocution, and **secondary anticoagulant rodenticide toxicosis**, a well documented and ongoing problem in this rodent-eating, human-landscape-tolerant species. Its status as the dominant nocturnal predator also matters in aviary design: Great Horned Owls are a significant predation threat to outdoor caging for other raptor patients and education birds.

## Diet and Biology

### Diet

The Great Horned Owl is a **generalist with one of the broadest diets of any North American owl**: across studies roughly 90 percent mammals and 10 percent birds, plus small numbers of reptiles, amphibians, and invertebrates. Staples are rabbits and hares, pocket gophers, ground squirrels, mice, voles, and rats; avian prey includes waterfowl and coots, roosting pigeons and starlings, and, notably, **other raptors (barn owls, screech-owls, long-eared owls, and nestling hawks)**. Skunks are taken regularly (a musky odor on an admitted bird is almost diagnostic of this species). Mean prey mass in California studies ranged from 28 g (northern California) to 98 g (central California). Carrion, including roadkill, is taken in harsh conditions, one route of rodenticide and lead exposure.

### Foraging Behavior

Primarily a **nocturnal perch hunter**, with activity peaks in the first half of the night and again before dawn, hunting from trees, poles, posts, and buildings adjacent to open ground; it also quarters low over grassland and will walk on the ground after invertebrates. Prey is detected by vision (large, tubular, rod-dominated eyes) and acute hearing. Small prey are swallowed whole, usually headfirst; larger prey are decapitated and dismembered, and headless carcasses are classic evidence of owl predation. Surplus prey is cached at the nest when food is abundant. Captive maintenance intake is roughly 15 to 27 g/kg/day; wild nestlings receive 166 to 411 g per nestling per day depending on prey abundance. Pellets are egested about once daily, with a meal-to-pellet interval of about 13 hours.

### Reproduction

This is the **earliest-nesting raptor in North America**: males begin advertising hooting in fall, and eggs are laid in late winter (most California clutches in February, with extremes from late January into March), when nights can still be freezing; females have successfully incubated at temperatures below minus 30 degrees C. Like all owls it **builds no nest**, instead using old stick nests of Red-tailed Hawks and other raptors, ravens, or squirrels, broken-off snags, tree cavities, cliff ledges, caves, and occasionally buildings or the ground; nests often disintegrate after a single season of use. The clutch is most commonly 2 dull-white, nearly spherical eggs (range 1 to 4). **Incubation by the female alone lasts about 33 days** (range 30 to 37), beginning with the first egg, so hatching is asynchronous. Hatchlings weigh about 35 to 50 g; the eyes open at 9 to 11 days. Young clamber onto branches ("branching") at about 6 weeks, fly at 7 weeks, and fledge at roughly 45 to 49 days at about 75 percent of adult mass. The post-fledging period is very long: parents feed the young for 2 to 3 months after fledging, begging continues into fall, and dispersal occurs from late September through January. Branchers found on the ground in spring are usually healthy and, when possible, should be re-nested or placed in a basket nest rather than admitted. Single brood per year; replacement clutches occur after early loss.

## Molt

The Great Horned Owl exhibits a **Complex Basic Strategy** with a partial preformative molt, incomplete prebasic molts, and no prealternate molt. There are 10 primaries, 16 to 17 secondaries (including 3 tertials), and 12 rectrices.

The preformative molt (May to November of the first year) replaces most body feathers but **no flight feathers**, so first-year birds keep a complete juvenile wing and tail. Subsequent prebasic molts are incomplete: typically 0 to 3 primaries centered on the nodal feather **p7** (the _Bubo_ starting point), 4 to 12 secondaries, and usually all rectrices are replaced each year, with replacement waves continuing in later years from where they arrested. As a result, juvenile primaries among p2 to p5 and p9 to p10 are commonly retained into the third and even fourth basic plumages, and adults carry 2 to 4 (up to 5) generations of basic remiges in seemingly random and often asymmetric positions. Practical ageing in the hand: uniform remiges equal first year; a block of fresh mid-outer primaries against worn, narrow, distinctly barred juvenile feathers equals second or third year; mixed basic generations without juvenile feathers equal adult. Molt occurs mainly July to October, after breeding.
