# Peregrine falcon

_Falco peregrinus_

## Description

### General Appearance

The Peregrine Falcon is a medium to large, powerfully built falcon (total length 36 to 49 cm in males, 45 to 58 cm in females; wingspan 79 to 114 cm) with long, pointed wings that reach nearly to the tail tip when perched. The **blackish malar ("moustache") stripe set off by pale cheeks** is the classic facial mark. Compared with the Prairie Falcon, the Peregrine is darker and more contrastingly patterned, with a proportionately shorter tail, and lacks the Prairie Falcon's dark axillary "wingpits"; its wingbeat is deeper and more fluid.

### Adult Plumage

Adults of the North American subspecies breeding in California (_F. p. anatum_) have **bluish-gray to slaty upperparts, blackish on the head**, whitish to buffy or tawny underparts with an unmarked upper breast and blackish spotting and barring below, heaviest on the flanks and thighs, and pale gray and black barring on the underwings and tail. Females average browner above and more heavily marked below.

### Juvenile Plumage

Juveniles are brown rather than blue-gray above (pale to slate or chocolate brown, with buffy feather edges) and buffy below with **vertical blackish streaking instead of the adult's transverse barring**, an at-a-glance ageing criterion. The malar stripe is usually narrower than in adults. Some resident California juveniles replace body feathers on the head, back, and breast during their first winter (formative molt), producing a mix of streaked and barred underparts.

### Differences Between Sexes

Plumage is similar between sexes, so size is the practical criterion: **females are 15 to 20 percent larger and 40 to 50 percent heavier than males, normally with no overlap within a subspecies**. For California _anatum_-type birds, males weigh roughly 500 to 700 g and females roughly 800 to 1,100 g.

### Bare Parts

The cere, orbital ring, and feet are **yellow to orange-yellow in adults and bluish-gray to greenish in juveniles**, another quick ageing feature; males are typically brighter, especially in the breeding season. The bill is slate blue, yellowish at the base with a black tip, and bears the tomial "tooth" characteristic of falcons. The iris is dark brown at all ages.

### Subspecies

The Peregrine is nearly cosmopolitan, with 19 or so subspecies worldwide; three occur in North America. **California breeders are _F. p. anatum_; the pale tundra-breeding _F. p. tundrius_ passes through and winters in the state, and the large, dark Pacific Northwest _F. p. pealei_ can occur along the coast in winter.** The eastern US population is a mixture of subspecies descended from reintroduced captive-bred stock.

## Distribution and Migration

### Overall Range

The Peregrine Falcon breeds on every continent except Antarctica, from arctic tundra to the tropics, and is among the most widespread birds in the world. Northern (arctic and subarctic) populations are long-distance migrants, some traveling to South America, whereas temperate and southern breeders, including California birds, are largely resident. The species famously crashed across North America and Europe from the 1950s to 1970s due to **DDT/DDE-induced eggshell thinning** (reduced productivity generally appeared where shell thinning exceeded about 18 percent), was extirpated from the eastern United States, and recovered through pesticide bans and one of the largest captive-breeding and release programs in conservation history.

### California

California's breeding population fell from **more than 100 known pairs historically to only 5 pairs in the early 1970s**, then recovered dramatically with DDT restriction and releases of captive-bred birds; the species was removed from the federal endangered species list in 1999 and from the California list in 2009, and remains a **California Fully Protected species**. Peregrines now breed on coastal cliffs and offshore islands (Channel Islands), inland cliffs including Yosemite, and increasingly on human structures: city high-rises, bridges, power plants, and quarries in the Bay Area, Los Angeles, San Diego, and Sacramento. Egg laying begins mid to late February in southern California and typically May in northern California, with replacement clutches as late as September. Urban nesting is now a routine source of fledgling admissions ("grounded" urban fledglings), along with collision trauma.

## Diet and Biology

### Diet

The Peregrine is an **aerial bird specialist: birds make up roughly 77 to 99 percent of the diet by frequency**, with at least 429 bird species documented as prey in North America, about 190 of them in California alone. Pigeons and doves are the most important prey nearly everywhere, supplemented regionally by shorebirds, waterfowl, seabirds (grebes, storm-petrels, alcids on the California coast and Channel Islands), swifts, and passerines. Bats are the most frequent mammalian prey. Males typically take birds of 20 to 300 g, females 100 to 1,000 g, with most prey in the 50 to 500 g range. Peregrines routinely **cache surplus prey** in crevices near the eyrie. The pigeon-heavy diet makes trichomonosis a recurrent clinical concern in this species.

### Foraging Behavior

Hunting is a sequence of search (usually from a high perch, sometimes from soaring flight), attack, capture, and killing. Attack modes include the famous **stoop, a dive of up to 1,000 m at speeds of 25 to 100 m/s**, plus ringing up under high-flying prey, direct tail-chasing pursuit, low contour-hugging surprise flights, and shepherding of tight flocks until a straggler breaks away. Prey is either bound (grabbed) or struck with splayed toes at speed, and is dispatched with a bite through the cervical vertebrae using the tomial teeth, the classic falcon killing bite. Average hunting success across 23 studies was about 24 percent, higher in breeding adults and lower in immatures. Urban pairs have been documented hunting at night by city lights. Captive males need roughly 80 to 140 g of food daily depending on temperature (about 12 to 26 percent of body mass); digestive efficiency is about 75 percent. Falcons regularly ingest small stones (rangle), a normal finding on imaging.

### Reproduction

Peregrines do not build a nest: the pair makes a **scrape** in dirt, sand, or gravel on a sheltered cliff ledge (traditionally one-third to halfway down cliffs of 50 to 200 m), in an old corvid or cormorant nest, or on ledges and nest boxes on buildings and bridges. Eyries are highly traditional, with some cliff sites reoccupied across decades. Clutch size averages 3 to 4 (mean about 3.7 at mid latitudes; occasionally 5 to 6), laid at about 48 hour intervals. **Incubation is 33 to 35 days**, shared, with the male covering roughly a third of daylight incubation. Hatchlings weigh 35 to 40 g. Young fledge at **35 to 42 days**, are fed by parents through a dependency of 5 to 6 weeks (migratory populations) to 9 to 10 weeks (residents), and develop from fluttering "butterfly flight" to powered flight over their first weeks; aerial food transfers and dropped-prey exchanges with parents are part of normal development, worth remembering when evaluating whether an urban fledgling truly needs rescue. Pairs renest within about 2 weeks if a clutch is lost. A useful aging formula for nestlings: age in days equals (wing chord in cm + 0.84) divided by 0.69.

## Molt

Unlike accipitrids, **falcons normally complete their flight-feather molt every year**, and their sequence differs fundamentally: **primary molt proceeds in both directions from p4** (typically p4, p5, p3, p6, p7, p2, p8, p1, p9, p10), secondaries from s5 in both directions, and rectrices from the central pair outward (r1, r2, r3, r6, r4, r5). This is diagnostic when reading a falcon wing in the clinic and matters for imping and feather-replacement planning.

The prejuvenile molt is complete in the nest, with the bird nearly fully feathered and capable of weak flight at about 40 days. There is no extensive first-year molt of flight feathers, but some resident California juveniles molt body feathers of the head, back, and breast in their first winter (formative molt). The definitive prebasic molt begins in the second year: residents may start as early as April, arctic migrants in mid to late June, with females starting about a week before males and northern females interrupting molt during brood rearing. Temperate birds finish in about 4.5 to 5 months (by September), whereas long-distance migrants may suspend molt and complete the outer primaries and tail on the wintering grounds as late as January, 8 months after starting.
